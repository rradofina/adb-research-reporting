# Related literature and the evidence gap

`attestation_chain: ai-first`

Three literatures motivate the comparison. First, work on health-facility
master lists shows that planning-quality inventories normally require
triangulation and continuing source reconciliation rather than reliance on a
single list [@maina2019facilities; @south2021reproducible;
@macharia2025mapping]. Second, research on administrative data warns that
official records can contain systematic incentives and measurement errors, so
an official registry should not be treated as automatic ground truth
[@sandefur2015badata; @markhof2025records]. Third, global OpenStreetMap work
documents spatially uneven completeness, particularly outside heavily mapped
areas [@herfort2023osm].

The broader health-information literature supplies a multidimensional data-
quality frame: completeness, accuracy, timeliness, consistency, and usability
are different properties [@ghalavand2024dataquality; @lemma2020scoping]. The
current study estimates a completeness disagreement in counts and geography.
It does not validate operating status, service readiness, or facility quality.

The marginal contribution is a reproducible Asia-Pacific public-data test that
places national regulatory registries and OpenStreetMap into the same
administrative comparison for the Philippines and Bangladesh, then carries the
disagreement into granular source-coverage and validation-priority modules.
The study also makes the non-resolution visible: targeted public-source review
did not establish the row-level identities needed to call either list correct.

The systematic search record, ten included studies, PRISMA-lite flow,
screening decisions, and full marginal-contribution discussion remain in
`literature.md`.

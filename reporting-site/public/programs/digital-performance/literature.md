# Related literature and evidence gap

`attestation_chain: ai-first` · Finalized 2026-07-19 under §18

A mobile network can cover almost everyone while many people still do not go
online. The literature treats network availability, internet use, barriers to
adoption, and the conversion of digital engagement into outcomes as related
but separate objects. What remains thin is an exact-year availability-versus-use
crosswalk for ADB developing member economies with source provenance and a
sample-floor check.

## Search protocol and scope

This is a focused, reproducible framing review rather than a claim of exhaustive
systematic coverage. Searches were run on 2026-07-19 before the paper was
finalized. The review retained sources that directly clarify at least one of
four constructs: network availability, internet use, barriers to adoption, or
the conversion of digital engagement into outcomes.

Included sources had a stable institutional landing page or DOI, a clear
construct definition or empirical design, and a direct bearing on the paper's
claim boundary. Excluded material consisted of vendor marketing, composite
readiness rankings, uncited commentary, speed-only studies without an adoption
denominator, and outcome studies that could not distinguish access from use.
The stopping rule fired when additional searches repeated the access–skills–use
framework without identifying an existing exact-year ADB-DMC availability-use
crosswalk with source-provenance and sample-floor sensitivity.

## Tier A — policy and statistical frameworks

The relevant literature does not treat connectivity as one variable. The ITU
framework for universal and meaningful connectivity separates infrastructure
from affordability, devices, skills, and safety [@itu2022globalconnectivity].
The World Bank's *Digital Dividends* report likewise argues that diffusion of
technology does not by itself produce development benefits; skills,
competition, institutions, and accountability shape conversion from access to
outcomes [@worldbank2016digitaldividends].

ADB's last-mile work places affordability, business models, regulation, and
rural or remote delivery beside physical connectivity
[@adb2022lastmileconnectivity]. Its 2025 regional policy report makes digital
inclusion and the distribution of benefits central to transformation rather
than assuming rollout is sufficient [@adb2025digitaltransformation]. ADBI's
regional synthesis similarly treats awareness, skills, institutions, and
sustainability as connected but distinct policy layers
[@adbi2024digitaltransformation]. These sources establish the operational
relevance of showing availability and use side by side, but they do not license
the aggregate subtraction as a count of covered non-users.

### Coverage and use are separate statistical objects

The GSMA literature distinguishes the mobile-broadband coverage gap—people
outside network range—from the usage gap—people within range who do not use
mobile internet [@gsma2024mobileconnectivity]. The present study follows that
conceptual separation but uses a more conservative estimand. ITU `i271GA` and
`i99H` are separate national indicators, not a person-level partition, so their
difference is described as an availability–use measurement gap rather than a
count of covered non-users.

ITU's 2024 evidence shows why the distinction matters. Internet use, coverage,
affordability, location, gender, age, and skills appear as separate statistical
dimensions [@itu2024factsfigures]. The corresponding DataHub definitions state
that coverage is signal range irrespective of use, while internet use means use
from any location in the previous three months [@itu2026datahub].

## Tier B — digital-divide constructs and measurement

The digital-divide literature moved beyond a binary access/no-access framing
well before current mobile-coverage debates. Van Dijk's review describes
physical access as one stage in a sequence that also includes skills, usage,
and participation [@vandijk2006divide]. Hargittai demonstrates substantial
variation in online skills even among people already classified as users
[@hargittai2002secondlevel]. These are construct warnings for the present
study: a high coverage estimate and even an internet-use response say little
about what a person can do online or the quality of that engagement.

Scheerder, van Deursen, and van Dijk's systematic review separates determinants
of skills, uses, and outcomes and documents why the second- and third-level
divides require different measurements [@scheerder2017determinants]. Helsper's
corresponding-fields model links digital exclusion to economic, cultural,
social, and personal resources through access, skills, attitudes, motivation,
relevance, quality, and sustainability [@helsper2012corresponding]. Lutz's
review shows that newer data-intensive technologies do not make those layered
inequalities disappear [@lutz2019inequalities]. Together these studies rule
out interpreting the national availability-use difference as a one-mechanism
adoption model.

## Tier C — adoption mechanisms and Asia-facing evidence

Cross-country work models accessibility, affordability, ease of use, and
perceived usefulness as distinct inputs to internet diffusion
[@zhang2013internetconsumption]. Rural South Asia survey evidence identifies
education and English-language knowledge as important correlates of computer
and internet use [@zhou2011ruralsouthasia]. Earlier urban–rural work in China
also emphasizes affordability and education as mechanisms behind uneven ICT
adoption [@fong2009urbanrural].

These studies warn against a one-variable explanation. A national price basket
can test whether more expensive mobile data moves with the availability–use
difference, but it cannot measure household affordability or isolate skills,
devices, local content, social norms, or perceived value.

The regional evidence also explains why the paper treats the ten-economy
urban-rural correlation as a hypothesis-generating diagnostic. Rurality can
bundle infrastructure, device, education, income, language, and relevance
constraints. The current aggregate panel does not identify which bundle is
operating in any economy, and reporting availability selects the diagnostic
sample.

## Synthesis and evidence gap addressed here

Existing global reports establish that coverage and use differ. This paper adds
a transparent, exact-year ADB developing-member crosswalk with five features:

1. a prospective headline-year rule;
2. no temporal imputation;
3. retained national source strings and notes;
4. an explicit denominator and missingness audit; and
5. a chart-forward claim gate that keeps speed, quality, affordability, and
   welfare outside the primary measure.

The contribution is regional statistical communication and reproducibility,
not a new causal theory of internet adoption.

## What this review changes in the empirical design

1. The headline reports both components and the signed difference; it never
   labels the difference a person-level mobile usage gap.
2. The 5 GB basket is a secondary association, not the affordability mechanism.
3. Rural/urban use is reported with its ten-case denominator and is not
   generalized to the full roster.
4. Speed-test data are reserved for performance conditional on testing.
5. Future explanation requires independent household, device, skills,
   attitudes, non-use-barrier, and distribution-sensitive price evidence.

# Deep questions and answers

`attestation_chain: ai-first`

## What is actually invisible?

The data do not observe legal or political invisibility. They observe two
measurement gaps: a national-definition share differs from a harmonized share,
and urban-cell population can sit inside a rural-classified administrative
unit. The paper uses “invisible urbanization” as the program topic, not as an
empirically proven legal status.

## Which measure is right?

Neither is universally right. WDI is appropriate for nationally defined urban
reporting. GHSL is appropriate for a comparable grid-based view. A divergence
is a prompt to investigate why the definitions differ.

## Why can finer units show more embedded urban population?

GHS-DUC assigns a class from the population composition inside each unit. A
large unit that contains a dominant city may be non-rural, so all its smaller
urban clusters disappear from the embedded category. At finer levels, some of
those clusters fall inside separate units whose overall composition remains
rural.

## Why does the embedded share fall over time?

Partly because units cross the standardized classification threshold. From
2000 to 2020, growth in persistently rural units added 13.9 million urban-cell
residents, but rural-to-town/city transitions removed 43.3 million from the
embedded stock. A smaller stock is therefore not equivalent to less urban
growth.

## What would turn this into a policy paper?

A country study needs official classification vintages, historical boundaries,
settlement-scale GHSL grids, and one outcome tied to recognition—such as
municipal finance, infrastructure coverage, or service jurisdiction. Without
that join, the paper remains a measurement study.
